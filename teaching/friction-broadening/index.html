Why smoothing can create a false dip

Electronic friction describes how moving atoms lose energy to electrons. This tool shows how a numerical smoothing step can make that friction look too small near zero frequency—even when the starting spectrum is completely flat.

The density of states (DOS) counts electronic states at each energy. At zero temperature, states below the Fermi level are occupied and those above it are empty. Here the Fermi level is zero.

An electron can absorb energy E if there is an occupied starting state and an empty destination E higher. The electron–hole JDOS counts these possibilities. It is not the same curve as the DOS—or the friction.

This is a simple continuum model, not a band-structure calculation. We assume constant coupling between all allowed pairs of states, with no momentum selection rule. A DOS alone cannot determine a material’s friction: real couplings and the requirement to conserve crystal momentum can change the result. In particular, this is not a prediction for a purely interband Γ-point calculation.

The short derivation

Set the Fermi level to zero. For E > 0, the initial electron energy ε lies between −E and 0. For each starting energy, multiply the number of occupied starting states by the number of empty final states:

Jeh(E)=∫−E0ρ(ε)ρ(ε+E)dε
Λ(E)=g2Jeh(E)E

ρ (rho) is the DOS. Common physical prefactors are suppressed, so plotted strengths are in arbitrary units. For a DOS continuous at the Fermi level, the limit is Λ(0) = g²ρ(0)². This follows by shrinking the integration interval while dividing by its width E.

For a flat DOS of height ρ₀, and excitation energies smaller than W, the integral is simply height squared times interval width: Jeh(E) = ρ₀²E. Dividing by E gives constant friction, g²ρ₀². The JDOS goes to zero while its ratio to E stays finite. Above W the finite band reduces the available phase space; beyond 2W there are no transitions.

The peaked DOS adds a Gaussian feature of height twice the background. Its width describes an electronic DOS feature, not numerical broadening. The gapped DOS removes states within half the chosen gap on each side of zero. Zero temperature is fixed in this example.

The lesson to remember

A spectrum is a picture of how much different energies contribute. In a calculation, those contributions may arrive as separate sharp peaks. Gaussian broadening means replacing each sharp peak with a smooth bell curve. The width σ tells us how far each contribution spreads.

Zero is the edge of the part we usually plot, but it is not the edge of the full friction response. If we discard the negative-frequency partners before smoothing, we also discard their tails. The apparent dip near zero can therefore come from the calculation, rather than the material.

The simplest test is a flat spectrum. Imagine that its true height is 1 at every energy, on both sides of zero. Ordinary Gaussian smoothing should still give 1 everywhere. At zero, the three methods give:

Λpositive only(0)=12=0.500
Λrescaled(0)=ln(2)≈0.693
Λboth partners(0)=1

Here Λ (the Greek letter lambda) is the friction strength. The argument (0) means “read the curve at zero.” ln(2) is the natural logarithm of 2.

Why rescaling falls short: it restores the total area above zero for each individual peak, but puts that weight in different places from the missing partner. For the flat example, it recovers about 69% of the zero-frequency value, rather than 100%. It can even produce a small bump further from zero. Conserving area is not enough to preserve the spectrum.

Try a peaked or gapped spectrum next. Smoothing will then change the shape even when both partners are included. That is a separate effect from the missing-weight problem at zero.

The equations, code settings, and limits of this example

One peak: two ways to restore its area

Let G be a Gaussian bell curve with total area 1, E the energy where we read the curve, and Δ the positive peak position. Let A be the fraction of the orange peak’s area lying above zero. The purple and orange-plus-green curves are:

Krescaled(E)=G(E−Δ)A
Kboth partners(E)=G(E−Δ)+G(E+Δ)

Both have area 1 above zero. The first multiplies a single curve by a constant; the second adds a different curve. That is why their shapes differ. The top plot adds these contributions over all positive peak positions, weighted by the spectrum you choose.

The connection to FHI-aims

friction_delta_normalisation .true. uses the rescaling prescription in the scalar tensor calculation. friction_negative_energies .true. adds the negative partners; use it with delta normalization off. This tool compares the prescriptions separately. The current tensor-grid routines do not apply the normalization flag.

The rescaling follows the positive-area integral described in Maurer et al. (2016), Eq. (23). Written using a positive peak position Δ and width σ, that area is:

A=12[1+erf(Δ2σ)]

The error function, erf, is simply a way to calculate Gaussian areas. This expression matches the current scalar code and this tool. The final error-function form printed in the paper has a sign/width inconsistency with its integral as written; using the positive peak position explicitly avoids that ambiguity.

What this example assumes

We model the real, even single-delta electronic friction response at q = 0, with the occupation difference divided by the electronic energy difference: see Box, Stark & Maurer (2023), Eq. (14). Exchanging the electronic states reverses both differences, leaving equal partner weights. This is not a rule to add negative phonon peaks to the Eliashberg function α²F, and it does not address diagonal or degenerate-state terms.

The flat test assumes a continuous spectrum. A real calculation also needs enough electronic states and k-points. The energies are in eV; friction strength is in arbitrary units. The inputs continue beyond the plotted window so that an upper boundary does not create a second artificial dip.

For reproducibility: the peaked preset adds a Gaussian of width 0.07 eV to the background; the gap uses a smooth step of scale 0.025 eV; the rising preset is background × (1 + Δ / 1 eV). Integration uses midpoint sampling with spacing no larger than σ/45 and extends eight Gaussian widths beyond 1 eV.
