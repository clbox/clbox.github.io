1 · Density of states

Which electronic states are available?

The density of states (DOS) counts electronic states at each energy. The Fermi level is our zero of energy. At zero temperature, states below it are occupied and states above it are empty. At higher temperature, the Fermi–Dirac function f gives the probability that each state is occupied.

Start with a flat band, then try adding a peak or opening a gap. Changes here update the JDOS and friction below, and the broadening example whenever its input is set to your DOS model.

f(ε)=11+exp[ε−μkBT]

T is electronic temperature in kelvin and kBT is the thermal energy scale. We keep the chemical potential μ fixed at zero as temperature changes. For an asymmetric DOS, this does not preserve the total electron number; a fixed-electron-number calculation would also solve for μ(T). The zero-temperature setting uses an exact occupation step.

What the DOS controls mean

The peaked DOS adds a Gaussian feature twice the background height. Its width describes a feature of the electronic states, not numerical smearing. The gapped DOS removes states within half the chosen gap on each side of zero. W is the band half-width, so the total band width is 2W.

2 · Electron–hole joint density of states

How many excitations are possible?

An upward transition needs an occupied lower state and an empty higher state. At finite temperature the reverse can also occur: an electron in a higher state can move into a hole below it. The net excitation weight is upward minus downward. This is the quantity used for friction; it is not a bare count of state pairs.

Upward J↑Downward J↓Net Jnet = J↑ − J↓

At T = 0, downward transitions vanish and the upward window is −E to 0. At finite T, occupations are gradual, and both directions contribute. Their occupation factors subtract to f(ε) − f(ε + E).

Jnet(E)=∫ρ(ε)ρ(ε+E)[f(ε)−f(ε+E)]dε

ρ (rho) is the DOS; ε is the starting electron energy. We integrate over electronic energies in the band, weighting each state pair by the occupation difference. The horizontal axis here is an energy difference, unlike the electron-energy axis in section 1.

Upward and downward are probabilities; friction uses their difference

The upward weight uses f(ε)[1−f(ε+E)]. The downward weight uses f(ε+E)[1−f(ε)]. Both are multiplied by the same DOS product and integrated over ε. At equilibrium and T > 0, J↓(E) = exp[−E/(kBT)] J↑(E).

At E = 0 and finite temperature, these two weights can each be nonzero but are equal: their net is zero. These are continuous spectral densities, not a count of discrete zero-energy transitions. The net weight’s slope, rather than the individual upward weight divided by E, determines zero-frequency friction.

Why a DOS is not enough for a real material

This is a continuum model with no momentum selection rule. In a crystal, the two states must also satisfy crystal-momentum conservation, so multiplying two DOS curves is generally not the actual momentum-resolved JDOS. This example illustrates the energy and occupation counting, not a particular material’s band structure.

3 · The friction spectrum

A vanishing JDOS can still give finite friction

In the single-delta friction expression considered here, the net excitation weight is divided by its energy E. For this model we use a constant coupling strength g², which describes how strongly atomic motion interacts with the electronic transitions.

Λ(E)=g2Jnet(E)E

How this tool calculates the point at zero

For E > 0, we calculate the net excitation weight and divide by E. At E = 0, we use the analytically derived continuum limit below, evaluating its integral numerically when T > 0. We do not evaluate 0/0 or extrapolate from plotted points.

Λ(0,T)=g2∫ρ2(ε)[−dfdε]dε

The occupation-difference quotient approaches −df/dε. At finite T this averages ρ² over a thermal window around μ. At T = 0 it selects only the Fermi level, recovering Λ(0) = g²ρ(0)² for a DOS continuous there. Constant coupling and no momentum selection are still assumed.

The plotted zero point follows from these model assumptions. It is not a direct evaluation of an exact zero-energy transition, or evidence that a finite discrete FHI-aims interband sum has the same limit.

For a wide flat DOS, the net excitation weight is approximately proportional to E, so dividing by E gives nearly flat friction. For a peak or gap, temperature can change the result by sampling different electronic states. Common physical prefactors are omitted, so friction is in arbitrary units.

Limits, Γ-point calculations, and negative frequencies

A DOS alone cannot determine physical friction. We assume constant coupling between all allowed pairs, equilibrium Fermi occupations at fixed chemical potential, and no momentum restriction. Real matrix elements and band selection rules can change the low-energy limit.

A fixed, discrete, nondegenerate interband sum with nonzero transition gaps has zero friction as both the observation frequency and broadening go to zero. Our continuum example does not contradict that: it has arbitrarily low-energy transitions when the DOS is nonzero at the Fermi level. It is not a prediction for a purely interband Γ-point calculation.

For the real scalar friction response considered here, a transition’s negative-frequency partner has the same weight. Exchanging the electronic states reverses both the occupation difference and the energy denominator. These partners describe the two-sided response to real motion; they are not an extra set of populated electronic excitations or a claim of emission from an empty state.

The model illustrates the energy-difference-denominator expression in Box, Stark & Maurer (2023), Eq. (14). It does not address diagonal or degenerate-state terms, or prescribe how to broaden phonon peaks in the Eliashberg function α²F.

4 · Gaussian broadening

What changes when we smooth the spectrum?

Numerical calculations often replace sharp contributions with Gaussian bell curves. The width σ controls how far their weight spreads. Temperature above changes which electronic states are occupied; σ here is a separate numerical width. All three methods below use the same Gaussian; they differ in how they treat the boundary at zero.

Keeping only positive-frequency peaks omits the tails of their negative partners. Rescaling each positive peak restores its area above zero. Including both partners instead smooths the full even response. These operations need not give the same answer near zero.

Λ(0) means the friction at zero. ln(2) is the natural logarithm of 2. Changing σ changes the width of the artificial dip but not these ratios in the ideal flat example. For structured spectra, even full smoothing changes the shape.

This tests how we smooth a specified continuum spectrum. It does not prove that a particular material must have nonzero zero-frequency friction. At a fixed positive energy, the partner tails become negligible as the Gaussian width becomes sufficiently small.

Why the methods differ: look at one peak

Area means total weight; height means the contribution at one energy. Rescaling raises the orange curve everywhere, adding most weight near +Δ. Adding the green tail puts extra weight near zero. At zero the orange and green heights are equal, so adding the partner doubles that contribution. “100% area” alone does not guarantee the right height at zero.

The two ways of restoring weight

G is a Gaussian with total area 1, Δ is the positive peak position, and A is the fraction of that peak lying above zero. The two prescriptions are:

Krescaled(E)=G(E−Δ)A
Kboth partners(E)=G(E−Δ)+G(E+Δ)

Both have area 1 above zero. The first multiplies the positive peak by a constant; the second adds a different curve. Only the reflected treatment also preserves a flat spectrum at the boundary. Conserving each peak’s area does not fix the height at every observation energy.

A=12[1+erf(Δ2σ)]

The error function, erf, calculates the Gaussian area. This is the positive-domain normalization described in Maurer et al. (2016), Eq. (23), written with Δ explicitly positive and σ included. It matches the current scalar code. The final error-function form printed in that equation has a sign/width inconsistency with its integral as written.

We Gaussian-smooth the friction spectrum itself. Broadening the dissipative susceptibility first and then dividing by frequency is a different finite-width approximation. The usual double-delta Fermi-surface expression is also a different calculation and has no positive-excitation boundary to repair this way.
